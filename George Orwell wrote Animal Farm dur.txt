George Orwell wrote *Animal Farm* during the Second World War. He finished it around 1944, but publishers initially refused to publish it because Britain was allied with the Soviet Union, while the book clearly criticised Stalin and the Soviet system. It was finally published in 1945, after the war in Europe had ended, and became a huge success.

*Animal Farm* is an allegory of the Russian Revolution. The animals overthrow their human farmer because they want freedom and equality. The pigs then take control of the farm. Old Major represents the revolutionary ideas that inspire the animals; Napoleon represents Stalin; Snowball represents Trotsky; Squealer represents propaganda; Boxer represents the hardworking working class; and the dogs represent the secret police. The animals initially believe that they are creating an equal society, but the pigs gradually become a privileged ruling class.

The main point of the book is not simply that "power corrupts." Orwell's more specific argument is that a revolution can begin with genuine ideals but can later be captured by a new ruling group that claims to represent the revolution. The old rulers are removed, but another group eventually takes their place. The revolution therefore becomes a betrayal of its original purpose.

One of the most important symbols in the book is the wall on which the animals write the Seven Commandments. These commandments are supposed to protect equality. However, whenever a rule becomes inconvenient for the pigs, they quietly change it. The animals remember that the rule was different, but Squealer convinces them that their memory is wrong. Eventually, the famous commandment "All animals are equal" becomes "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others." Orwell is showing how a government can control people not only by controlling information, but also by changing the public record and making people doubt their own memory.

Squealer represents propaganda. Whenever Napoleon does something that goes against the original principles of the revolution, Squealer provides an explanation. If the pigs receive special privileges, there is a justification. If food becomes scarce, statistics are produced to show that conditions are supposedly improving. If someone is executed, they are declared a traitor. In this way, propaganda makes people accept actions that they would otherwise recognise as wrong.

Boxer represents the ordinary working class. He is extremely hardworking and loyal to the revolution, believing that he should simply work harder and trust Napoleon. But when Boxer becomes old and is no longer useful, Napoleon sells him instead of protecting him. This shows how a ruling elite can use ordinary workers while they are useful and abandon them when they are no longer needed.

At the end of the book, the pigs become so similar to the humans they originally fought against that the other animals can no longer tell the difference between them. This is the central message of the story: the revolution has come full circle. The animals removed one oppressive ruling class but ended up under another.

Orwell's choice to tell this political argument as a simple animal story was important. Instead of directly writing a political attack on Stalinism, he created a story that anyone could understand. The reader can follow the animals' struggle even without knowing the details of Soviet history. The story therefore makes the argument before the reader has a chance to turn it into an abstract political debate.

In simple terms, *Animal Farm* is about how a revolution can begin with equality and freedom but be betrayed by a new ruling elite that maintains its power through propaganda, manipulation of public memory, and gradual rewriting of the rules.
