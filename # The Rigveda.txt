# The Rigveda

## What is the Rigveda?

The **Rigveda**, or Ṛgveda, is one of the four Vedas and one of the oldest surviving bodies of religious poetry in the world. Its name comes from *ṛc*, meaning “praise,” and *veda*, meaning “knowledge.” It is composed in **Vedic Sanskrit**.

The Rigveda is a collection of hymns, or **sūktas**, addressed mainly to gods and other sacred powers. It is classified in Hindu tradition as **śruti**, meaning something that is “heard” and transmitted through an oral teacher-to-student tradition.

The Rigveda is generally dated to around **1500–1000 BCE**, although scholars have proposed wider dates, including approximately **1900–1200 BCE**. Its earliest layers are among the oldest surviving texts in any Indo-European language.

It contains:

* **10 maṇḍalas**, or books
* **1,028 hymns**
* about **10,600 verses**, called ṛcs

Only one major recension, or **śākhā**, the **Śākala Śākhā**, has survived in complete form. Much of the material belonging to other ancient schools has been lost or is no longer publicly available.

The Rigveda is not simply one book composed at one moment. It is a **layered collection**. Its associated literature includes the **Saṃhitā**, **Brāhmaṇas**, **Āraṇyakas**, and **Upaniṣads**.

---

# 1. How old is the Rigveda?

Dating the Rigveda is difficult and remains debated.

Most philological and linguistic estimates place the bulk of the Rigveda in the **second half of the second millennium BCE**, particularly around **1500–1000 BCE**. Other proposals extend the possible period from about **1900 to 1200 BCE**.

The hymns must have been composed after the separation of the Indo-Iranian languages, generally dated to around **2000 BCE**.

Another important comparison comes from the **Mitanni documents** of northern Syria and Iraq, dating to approximately **1450–1350 BCE**. These documents contain Indo-Aryan names and references to gods such as **Mitra, Varuna and Indra**, providing evidence that an Indo-Aryan language and religious vocabulary existed outside India by that time.

Some scholars have proposed that parts of the Rigveda were composed around the **Haraxvaiti** region of southern Afghanistan, whose name is related to Sanskrit *Sarasvati* and may refer to the Helmand or Arghandab river.

Other evidence has been interpreted as pointing toward a date around **1400 BCE**.

The earliest material appears to have been composed in the **northwestern Indian subcontinent**, while some of the later and more philosophical material was probably composed farther east, around what is now **Haryana**.

The Rigveda's core is therefore generally regarded as a **Late Bronze Age text**.

Michael Witzel dates its codification to approximately **1200–1000 BCE**, in the early Kuru kingdom. Asko Parpola argues that it was systematized around **1000 BCE**, also during the Kuru period.

### An important warning

There are **no surviving written records from the period when the Rigveda was composed**.

This does not mean that the text was poorly preserved.

Quite the opposite: scholars believe that the oral tradition preserved it with extraordinary precision because the exact pronunciation, sounds, accents and sequence of the words were themselves part of the tradition.

Michael Witzel compares the Vedic oral tradition to something like a **“tape-recording”** of the ancient text: not merely the words, but even its musical or tonal accent was preserved.

---

# 2. The Rigveda was primarily an oral text

This is one of the most important facts about the Vedas.

The Rigvedic hymns were **composed, memorized and transmitted orally**.

They were passed from teacher to student through highly controlled systems of memorization. The tradition did not simply require people to remember the general meaning. It preserved the **exact sounds, pronunciation, accents and sequence** of the text.

This was achieved through extremely complex methods of recitation.

The tradition developed detailed ways of:

* breaking words apart,
* identifying stems and grammatical endings,
* preserving pronunciation,
* applying and reversing *sandhi*,
* and arranging words in different patterns for memorization.

This oral discipline helped produce an ancient scholarly tradition concerned with **phonetics and morphology**.

The Rigveda's oral transmission continued into modern times.

Some scholars believe attempts to write the Vedas may have been made toward the **end of the 1st millennium BCE**, but such attempts may initially have failed or remained limited because traditions concerning the Vedas discouraged writing them down.

Witzel argues that the Vedas were written down much later than their composition. The uploaded source also notes that some Upanishadic material may have entered written form around the middle of the first millennium CE.

The **oldest surviving Rigveda manuscripts**, however, are much later: manuscripts discovered in Nepal date to approximately **1040 CE**.

This explains something that can otherwise seem strange:

> The Rigveda is thousands of years old, but the physical manuscripts we possess are medieval.

The reason is that the **text survived orally for centuries before the surviving manuscripts were copied**.

---

# 3. What you see today in Devanagari is not the original writing of the Rigveda

A modern reader might open the Rigveda and see:

**अग्निमीळे पुरोहितं**

It is easy to assume that the Rigveda was written in Devanagari.

It was not.

The Rigveda is an ancient **Vedic Sanskrit text**, while Devanagari is a much later script.

The manuscript shown on the first page of the source is itself an **early-19th-century Rigveda manuscript written in Devanagari**. It contains the opening verse:

*agniṃ iḷe puraḥ-hitaṃ yajñasya devaṃ ṛtvijaṃ*

meaning approximately:

> “I praise Agni, the chosen priest, god and minister of the sacrifice.”

The manuscript even marks the ancient pitch accent using red lines and marks.

So the Devanagari you see in modern editions is a **later written representation of a much older orally transmitted Sanskrit text**.

Rigveda manuscripts have been found in several scripts:

* **Devanagari** — Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Nepal
* **Grantha** — Tamil Nadu
* **Malayalam** — Kerala
* **Nandinagari** — South India
* **Sharada** — Punjab and Kashmir

The language in these manuscripts is Sanskrit, but the **script changes according to region and period**.

This is why it is important to distinguish:

> **Language ≠ script**

The Rigveda is in **Vedic Sanskrit**.

It can be represented using **Devanagari, Sharada, Grantha, Nandinagari, Malayalam**, and other scripts.

---

# 4. The Rigveda's structure

The central text is the **Rigveda Saṃhitā**.

It consists of **10 maṇḍalas**, or books.

The books are not all equally old.

### The oldest books

Maṇḍalas **2–7** are called the **Family Books**.

They are the oldest and shortest part of the Rigveda and account for about **38%** of the text.

They are associated with particular priestly families and clans.

| Maṇḍala | Main clan/family   | Region                                         |
| ------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 2       | Gṛtsamāda          | Northwestern region, Punjab                    |
| 3       | Viśvāmitra         | Punjab, Sarasvati                              |
| 4       | Vāmadeva           | Northwestern region, Punjab                    |
| 5       | Atri               | Northwestern region → Punjab → Yamuna          |
| 6       | Bharadvāja         | Northwestern region, Punjab, Sarasvati → Ganga |
| 7       | Vasiṣṭha           | Punjab, Sarasvati → Yamuna                     |
| 8       | Kaṇva and Āṅgirasa | Northwestern region                            |

Tradition associates individual verses with particular **ṛṣis**, or poet-sages. Ten families of ṛṣis account for more than **95% of the ṛcs**.

### The younger books

Maṇḍalas **1 and 10** are generally considered the youngest and together contain about **37% of the text**.

They contain more philosophical and speculative material.

However, some hymns in Books 1 and 8 may actually be very old, possibly as old as the Family Books.

The **10th Maṇḍala** is linguistically the latest overall and appears to have been added after the earlier books.

---

# 5. What is inside the hymns?

The earliest books, especially Books 2–9, concentrate heavily on:

* gods,
* sacrifices,
* cosmology,
* ritual,
* and praise of the deities.

Books 1 and 10 contain much more philosophical speculation.

They ask questions about:

* the origin of the universe,
* the nature of reality,
* the divine,
* human existence,
* charity,
* truth,
* morality,
* and the ultimate source of speech and creation.

This means the Rigveda is not simply a collection of prayers.

It contains **ritual poetry, mythology, philosophical speculation, riddles, dialogue poems, love stories, ethical ideas and cosmological questions**.

---

# 6. The organization of a hymn

Each maṇḍala contains **sūktas**, or hymns.

A sūkta is made up of individual verses called **ṛcs**.

The ṛcs are further divided into **padas**, or metrical units.

So:

**Maṇḍala → Sūkta → Ṛc → Pada**

The word *sūkta* literally conveys the idea of something **well recited or well spoken**.

The Rigveda uses several poetic metres.

The most important are:

* **Gāyatrī** — 3 × 8 syllables
* **Anuṣṭubh** — 4 × 8
* **Triṣṭubh** — 4 × 11
* **Jagatī** — 4 × 12

The **Triṣṭubh** metre accounts for about **40%** of the verses, while **Gāyatrī** accounts for about **25%**.

---

# 7. The two important ways of preserving the text

The Rigveda was transmitted in several forms.

Two particularly important ones are:

### Saṃhitāpāṭha

This is the continuous recitation of the text, with words joined according to the rules of **sandhi**.

It is the form used for recitation.

### Padapāṭha

Here words are separated into their individual forms.

This makes the grammatical structure easier to preserve and was used as a memorization aid.

The **Padapāṭha** and the **Prātiśākhya** traditions helped anchor the meaning and pronunciation of the text.

Through these techniques, the Rigveda was preserved with extraordinary fidelity for more than a millennium through oral transmission alone.

---

# 8. How was the Rigveda finally codified?

The Rigveda was not necessarily created as one finished book.

Its hymns belonged to different poetical families and traditions.

Around **1200 BCE**, according to the account associated with Michael Witzel, the material was collected and organized during the early Kuru period.

The political background may have been important.

According to Witzel, the collection followed the victory of **King Sudās** in the Battle of the Ten Kings and helped bring different tribal and priestly factions together under emerging Kuru-Bharata political power.

The collection was later reorganized and expanded in the Kuru kingdom.

This process also reflected changes in ritual practice.

The Kuru political order therefore appears to have played an important role in turning diverse oral traditions into a more standardized **Vedic canon**.

---

# 9. The development of the Vedic text continued after the original hymns

The text did not simply remain frozen after the hymns were composed.

Its transmission involved later **redaction**, additions and changes in pronunciation.

The regular application of **sandhi** to the Vedic chant and the creation of the **Padapāṭha** are associated with approximately the **6th century BCE**, during the later Brāhmaṇa period.

The surviving Rigveda therefore reflects several stages:

1. ancient hymns were composed;
2. different priestly families transmitted them orally;
3. they were collected and organized;
4. the Kuru period helped establish a standardized corpus;
5. later scholars fixed pronunciation and textual arrangements;
6. eventually the material was written into manuscripts.

The surviving text is therefore the result of a **long history of oral preservation, organization and redaction**, not the product of a single author writing a book.

---

# 10. The different Śākhās

A **śākhā** means a “branch” or school.

Different regions and schools once had their own versions of the Rigveda.

Only the **Śākala Śākhā** has survived completely.

The **Bāṣkala Śākhā** may also survive in part, although this is uncertain.

The Śākala recension contains:

* **1,017 regular hymns**
* an additional 11 Vālakhilya hymns
* **1,028 hymns in total**

The Bāṣkala recension contains:

* 1,025 regular hymns
* an appendix of **98 Khilani hymns**
* 1,123 hymns when those are included.

Other Rigvedic schools are mentioned in ancient sources, including:

* Māṇḍukāyana
* Aśvalāyana
* Śaṅkhāyana
* Saisiriya

Their differences appear to have been relatively small, involving the order of material and the inclusion or exclusion of particular verses.

But most of these traditions disappeared.

The surviving Rigvedic corpora are essentially those of **Śākala and Bāṣkala**.

---

# 11. How large is the Rigveda?

The 1,028 hymns contain approximately:

* **10,552 ṛcs**
* **39,831 padas**

Different calculations give different syllable counts.

The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa gives **432,000 syllables**, while the metrically analyzed text of van Nooten and Holland gives **395,563 syllables**, averaging about **9.93 syllables per pada**.

Counting syllables is complicated by **sandhi** and changes in pronunciation after the Rigvedic period.

The number therefore varies depending on how the text is analyzed.

---

# 12. The Rigveda and the other Vedas

The Rigveda is the **largest of the four Vedas**.

It also became the source for a large amount of material in the other Vedas.

Almost all of the **1,875 verses of the Samaveda** are taken from the Rigveda, either repeated directly or transformed into chant form.

The **8th and 9th maṇḍalas** are especially important sources for the Samaveda.

The Atharvaveda also contains many Rigvedic verses. Book 10 is particularly important as a source.

The Yajurveda, in its numerous versions, also borrows a large amount of ritual material from the Rigveda.

So the Rigveda is not merely one Veda among four isolated books. It is one of the **foundational textual sources of the wider Vedic corpus**.

---

# 13. The Rigveda's associated literature

The wider Rigvedic tradition consists of several layers:

### Saṃhitā

The collection of hymns. This is the oldest and central portion.

### Brāhmaṇas

Texts explaining and interpreting Vedic rituals.

Two Rigvedic Brāhmaṇas survive:

* **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa**
* **Kauṣītaki/Śāṅkhāyana Brāhmaṇa**

### Āraṇyakas

Literally “forest books.”

They contain ritual material as well as increasingly speculative discussions.

### Upaniṣads

Some Upanishadic material is embedded within or associated with the Āraṇyakas.

The Aitareya tradition contains the **Aitareya Upaniṣad**, while the Kauṣītaki tradition contains the **Kauṣītaki Upaniṣad**.

---

# 14. The Aitareya and Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇas

The **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa** and **Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa** preserve related ritual traditions but differ in arrangement and style.

The Kauṣītaki is more concise and systematic and is therefore generally considered the more recent of the two.

It has **30 chapters**.

The Aitareya has **40 chapters**, arranged in eight groups of five.

Its final ten chapters appear to be later additions, although they were already known by the time of **Pāṇini**, usually dated to around the 5th century BCE.

The Aitareya focuses heavily on the **Soma sacrifice**.

The Kauṣītaki discusses various forms of ritual offerings, including offerings of:

* rice,
* milk,
* ghee,

followed by Soma ritual and recitations performed by the **hotṛ** priest.

The Aitareya tradition is associated with the sage **Mahidāsa Aitareya**.

The authorship of the Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa is less certain, although the sage Kauṣītaki is repeatedly treated as an authority within it.

---

# 15. The Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads

Each of these Brāhmaṇas is supplemented by an Āraṇyaka.

The **Aitareya Āraṇyaka** contains five books.

Some parts are strongly ritualistic, especially discussions of the **Mahāvrata**, or “great vow.”

Other parts are speculative.

The last four chapters of its second book are usually identified as the **Aitareya Upaniṣad**.

The third book is also called the **Saṃhitā-Upaniṣad**.

The **Kauṣītaki Āraṇyaka** contains 15 chapters.

Parts of it correspond to sections of the Aitareya Āraṇyaka, while four chapters form the **Kauṣītaki Upaniṣad**.

Its remaining sections discuss subjects such as the vital breaths and the internal Agnihotra ritual, and conclude with a succession of teachers.

---

# 16. What gods appear in the Rigveda?

The Rigvedic hymns address many gods and sacred forces.

The most prominent include:

* **Indra**
* **Agni**
* **Soma**
* **Mitra**
* **Varuna**
* **Uṣas**
* **Savitṛ**
* **Viṣṇu**
* **Rudra**
* **Pūṣan**
* **Bṛhaspati/Brahmaṇaspati**
* **Sūrya**
* **Vāyu**
* **Parjanya**

The text also personifies natural phenomena such as:

* sky,
* earth,
* sun,
* wind,
* waters,
* thunder,
* rivers,
* speech.

The Rigveda mentions the **Sapta Sindhu** region and the **Sarasvati** river.

It also refers to groups such as the:

* Ādityas
* Vasus
* Rudras
* Sādhyas
* Aśvins
* Maruts
* Ṛbhus
* Viśvedevas

and the traditional group of **thirty-three gods**.

---

# 17. The ten maṇḍalas

## Maṇḍala 1

Contains **191 hymns**.

The first hymn is addressed to **Agni**, making Agni the first named deity in the Rigveda.

The book also contains hymns to Indra, Varuna, Mitra, the Aśvins, Maruts, Uṣas, Sūrya, Rudra, Vāyu, Viṣṇu and other gods.

It contains the famous **Riddle Hymn 1.164**, which influenced later Upanishadic thought, including the Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad.

## Maṇḍala 2

Contains **43 hymns**, mainly to Agni and Indra.

It is chiefly associated with the ṛṣi **Gṛtsamada Śaunahotra**.

## Maṇḍala 3

Contains **62 hymns**.

It contains the famous verse **3.62.10**, the **Gāyatrī Mantra**.

Most of the hymns are associated with **Viśvāmitra**.

## Maṇḍala 4

Contains **58 hymns**, mainly concerning Agni, Indra and other deities.

Most hymns are associated with **Vāmadeva Gautama**.

## Maṇḍala 5

Contains **87 hymns**.

It addresses Agni, Indra, the Viśvedevas, Maruts, Mitra-Varuna, the Aśvins, Uṣas and Savitṛ.

It is particularly associated with the **Atri clan**.

## Maṇḍala 6

Contains **75 hymns**.

It is mainly associated with the **Bharadvāja** family.

## Maṇḍala 7

Contains **104 hymns**.

It includes hymns to Agni, Indra, the Viśvedevas, Maruts, Mitra-Varuna, Uṣas, Vāyu, Sarasvati and Viṣṇu.

It is mainly associated with **Vasiṣṭha**.

## Maṇḍala 8

Contains **103 hymns**.

It contains the Vālakhilya hymns, 8.49–8.59, which are sometimes regarded as later or apocryphal material.

Many hymns are associated with the **Kaṇva** and **Āṅgirasa** clans.

## Maṇḍala 9

Contains **114 hymns**, all devoted to **Soma Pavamāna**, the purified Soma drink.

## Maṇḍala 10

Contains **191 hymns** and is frequently written in a later form of language.

It contains some of the Rigveda's most famous philosophical and social hymns, including:

* the **Nadīstuti**, praising rivers;
* the **Puruṣa Sūkta**;
* the **Nāsadīya Sūkta**, or Creation Hymn;
* the marriage hymn **10.85**;
* and the death hymns **10.10–18**.

The Nāsadīya Sūkta asks whether anyone actually knows how the universe originated.

The marriage and death hymns remain important in corresponding Hindu domestic rituals.

---

# 18. The Rigveda's society

The Rigveda gives only limited direct evidence about the social and political organization of its society.

It mentions things such as:

* cattle raising,
* horse racing,
* agriculture,
* kings,
* priests,
* clans,
* and warfare.

The society appears to have been **semi-nomadic and pastoral**, although agriculture was known.

The hymns mention the plough and agricultural deities.

There was division of labor and a relationship between kings and poet-priests, but the text does not provide evidence for an elaborate, fully developed caste system.

Jamison and Brereton argue that social stratification appears **embryonic**, more an ideal than a fully established social reality.

---

# 19. Women in the Rigveda

Women appear prominently as speakers in several dialogue hymns.

The text contains female figures including:

* Indrāṇī
* Urvaśī
* Yamī
* Apālā Ātreyī
* Ghoṣā
* Lopāmudrā
* Viśvavārā Ātreyī
* Śacī Paulomī
* Śaśvatī Āṅgirasī

The Rigveda therefore preserves a surprisingly large number of female voices.

The women in its dialogue hymns can be outspoken and sexually confident.

The elaborate wedding hymns indicate that marriage rituals were already developed.

There is little evidence for dowry and **no evidence of sati** in the Rigveda or related Vedic texts.

---

# 20. Animals, food and technology

The Rigveda frequently mentions animals.

Important animals include:

* horses,
* cattle,
* sheep,
* goats,
* elephants,
* camels,
* asses,
* buffalo,
* wolves,
* hyenas,
* lions,
* mountain goats,
* and the gaur.

Birds include:

* peafowl,
* geese,
* and the chakravaka.

Rice and porridge are mentioned in several hymns, although the text does not discuss rice cultivation directly.

The word **ayas**, meaning metal, occurs, but it is unclear exactly which metal it refers to.

**Iron is not mentioned in the Rigveda.**

This absence has been used by scholars as one piece of evidence supporting a date before approximately **1000 BCE**.

A hymn refers to “metal cloaked in gold,” suggesting that metalworking was already developed.

---

# 21. Languages that influenced Rigvedic vocabulary

Most Rigvedic vocabulary belongs to Indo-Aryan and shares roots with other Indo-European languages.

The Rigveda also contains approximately **300 words** that Frits Staal considers neither Indo-Aryan nor Indo-European.

Some appear to come from **Munda or Proto-Munda/Austroasiatic languages**, particularly words such as:

* *kapardin*
* *kumara*
* *kumari*
* *kikata*

Others appear to have **Dravidian** roots, while some may come from **Tibeto-Burman** languages.

A few words, including terms associated with the **camel, mustard and donkey**, may belong to a now-lost Central Asian language.

This mixture suggests that the speakers of Rigvedic Sanskrit were already in contact with speakers of **Munda and Dravidian languages**.

---

# 22. The Rigveda and the Indo-European world

The language of the Rigveda is exceptionally important for historical linguistics.

Its grammar and vocabulary are highly archaic.

Its poetry has striking similarities with the oldest surviving poetic traditions of other Indo-European branches, particularly:

* the **Old Avestan Gāthās** of Iran;
* and **Homer's Iliad and Odyssey** in Greek.

The Rigveda and the Avesta also share numerous linguistic and cultural similarities.

This makes the Rigveda an extremely important source for reconstructing:

* Old Indo-Aryan,
* Indo-Iranian,
* and ultimately **Proto-Indo-European**.

Its archaic grammar and syntax preserve linguistic features that disappeared from later Sanskrit and related languages.

---

# 23. The Rigveda is not philosophically uniform

The oldest hymns largely reflect a sacrificial religious world in which gods such as Indra and Agni are praised and invoked through ritual.

The younger material, especially in Maṇḍalas 1 and 10, becomes much more speculative.

Scholars have described tendencies toward:

* polytheism,
* monotheism,
* henotheism,
* monism,
* pantheism,

and even passages that can be interpreted as having an atheistic tendency.

One famous example is **Rigveda 1.164.46**, which says that sages give many names to what is ultimately One.

Max Müller used the term **henotheism** to describe this type of religious thinking, partly because “monotheism” carried assumptions from the Judeo-Christian context.

---

# 24. The Nāsadīya Sūkta: the Creation Hymn

One of the most famous passages is **Rigveda 10.129**, the Nāsadīya Sūkta.

It asks what existed before the universe.

It describes a state in which there was neither existence nor non-existence, neither death nor immortality, and no clear distinction between night and day.

It then asks:

**Where did creation come from?**

The gods themselves came **after** creation, so how could even the gods know its origin?

The hymn ends with an extraordinary possibility:

Perhaps the highest being knows how creation happened.

Or perhaps **even it does not know**.

The hymn is one of the major roots of later Indian philosophical speculation and is among the most frequently discussed Rigvedic hymns in modern presentations.

---

# 25. Other philosophical questions

The younger Rigvedic hymns ask questions such as:

* What is the ultimate limit of the earth?
* What is the center of the universe?
* Where does the sun go at night?
* Where do the gods live?
* What supports the universe?
* How did an ordered world emerge from an unstructured one?
* What is the ultimate source of human speech?

One of the most famous images appears in **1.164.20**:

Two birds sit together on the same tree.

One eats from the tree.

The other does not eat; it simply watches.

This image later became important in Upanishadic thought as a metaphor for the **body and the soul**.

---

# 26. Ṛta and the development of later Hindu thought

The Rigveda contains the important concept of **ṛta**.

It represents an active realization of truth and a form of **cosmic order or harmony**.

The concept later contributed to the development of the Hindu idea of **dharma**.

The Rigveda also contains statements emphasizing:

* truthful speech,
* truthful action,
* self-discipline,
* righteousness,
* charity,
* generosity.

Hymn **10.117**, for example, discusses the importance of generosity and helping people in need.

Some hymns describe the human desire for gain, ease, freedom, joy and satisfaction, themes that later appear in Upanishadic discussions of self-realization.

---

# 27. The meaning of Śruti

In Hindu tradition, the Vedas are called **śruti**, literally “that which is heard.”

This is sometimes compared with the idea of divine revelation in Western religions.

However, Frits Staal emphasizes that *śruti* itself simply refers to something heard and transmitted through a teacher-to-student or father-to-son tradition.

The Rigveda does not explicitly state that it is **apauruṣeya**, meaning not authored by human beings.

The concept of the Veda's timeless, authorless status became important only later, particularly in the **Mīmāṃsā** philosophical tradition.

The Rigveda itself contains the names of poet-composers, and its hymns appear to have been understood historically as works of particular human poets.

---

# 28. How the Rigveda was interpreted

The ancient scholar **Yāska**, usually dated to around the 4th century BCE, was an early commentator.

In his work **Nirukta**, he discussed difficult words in the Rigveda.

He identified several ways of interpreting the text:

1. **Adhiyajña** — from the perspective of ritual;
2. **Adhidevatā** — from the perspective of the gods;
3. **Adhyātman** — from the perspective of the self or soul.

Another interpretive approach treated the gods as symbols for legendary people or narratives.

The Rigvedic poets were also understood to use:

* double meanings,
* ellipses,
* wordplay,
* and deliberately novel ideas.

By the medieval period, however, the language of the hymns had become extremely difficult for ordinary readers to understand.

In the period of **Purāṇic Hinduism**, interpretation increasingly relied on mystical ideas and symbolism connected with sound.

---

# 29. Vyāsa, Śākala and the traditional account

Later Hindu tradition attributes the compilation of the four Vedas to **Ved Vyāsa**.

According to the Purāṇic tradition, Vyāsa taught the Rigveda Saṃhitā to **Paila**, who began the oral transmission.

Another tradition says that **Śākala** compiled the Rigveda from the teachings of the Vedic ṛṣis.

These are traditional accounts rather than modern historical reconstructions.

---

# 30. Medieval commentators

The Rigveda continued to receive commentary long after its original composition.

**Madhvācārya**, a 13th-century philosopher, wrote a commentary on the first 40 hymns.

The most important medieval commentator was **Sāyaṇa**, who lived in the 14th century.

Sāyaṇa produced an extensive commentary on the entire Rigveda.

Other medieval commentators included:

* Skandasvāmin,
* Udgītha,
* Veṅkaṭa-Mādhava,
* and Mudgala.

Sāyaṇa's commentary became particularly influential because later European scholars used it extensively when attempting to understand the Rigveda.

---

# 31. How the Rigveda entered modern scholarship

The Rigveda is extraordinarily difficult to translate.

The difficulties come from several factors:

* its enormous length,
* its poetic language,
* the extreme antiquity of its Sanskrit,
* and the absence of closely contemporary texts that can be used for comparison.

Frits Staal described it as one of the most obscure and difficult ancient texts for modern readers.

Early translators sometimes made straightforward errors.

There is also a danger of interpreting the text according to theories that scholars already expect to find in it.

Even apparently simple terms can be misleading. For example, *maṇḍala* is conventionally translated as “book,” but its more literal meaning is closer to **“circle.”**

Karen Thomson has argued that much of the apparent obscurity results from layers of traditional assumptions about ritual meaning being imposed on the text.

---

# 32. The first European translations

The first published European translation of part of the Rigveda was into **Latin**, by **Friedrich August Rosen**.

Rosen worked from manuscripts brought to Europe by **Henry Thomas Colebrooke**.

In **1849**, Max Müller published a major German translation.

In **1850–1888**, **H. H. Wilson** published the first English translation.

Wilson's translation relied heavily on the 14th-century commentary of Sāyaṇa.

Later translations appeared in:

* German,
* French,
* Russian,
* English,
* Hindi,
* Japanese,
* Kannada,
* and other languages.

**Karl Friedrich Geldner** produced an important scholarly German translation in the 1920s, which was published after his death.

The source also records translations by:

* A. Langlois,

# 33. Why translations can disagree

A major problem is that the Rigveda has no close contemporary Indian text that can simply be placed beside it and used as a dictionary or parallel translation.

Its language is much older than Classical Sanskrit.

The meaning of some words therefore has to be reconstructed using:

* grammar,
* comparative Indo-European linguistics,
* metre,
* context,
* later Sanskrit,
* Avestan,
* and the traditional commentaries.

Different translators can consequently arrive at different interpretations.

The source notes that some selective anthologies can give a distorted impression because they choose only certain hymns.

In **1994**, Barend A. van Nooten and Gary B. Holland produced an attempt to restore the entire Rigveda to its poetic form by identifying sound changes and sandhi combinations that had affected the apparent metre and meaning.

---

# 34. The manuscripts we actually possess

This returns us to the question of writing.

The Rigveda was orally transmitted for centuries, but eventually manuscripts were produced.

The manuscripts were written on materials such as:

* **birch bark**
* **palm leaves**
* later **paper**

These materials decay, so manuscripts had to be repeatedly copied.

One important collection is held at the **Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute**.

Thirty Rigveda manuscripts were collected in the 19th century by scholars including **Georg Bühler and Franz Kielhorn**.

They came from different parts of India, including:

* Kashmir,
* Gujarat,
* Rajputana,
* the Central Provinces.

The oldest manuscript in that Pune collection dates to **1464 CE**.

The collection was added to UNESCO's **Memory of the World International Register in 2007**.

The manuscripts are written in **Sharada and Devanagari**.

One manuscript, written in bold Sharada on birch bark, was used partly by Max Müller for his edition.

Müller had access to 24 manuscripts in Europe. The Pune edition used more than 60 manuscripts. The known number of surviving manuscripts at that time therefore probably exceeded 80.

---

# 35. Why the surviving manuscripts look so different

Rigveda manuscripts from different parts of India use different scripts.

A manuscript from Kashmir might use **Sharada**.

A manuscript from northern India might use **Devanagari**.

A manuscript from Tamil Nadu might use **Grantha**.

A manuscript from Kerala might use **Malayalam**.

A South Indian manuscript might use **Nandinagari**.

Yet all of them can preserve the **same Sanskrit text**.

This is an important distinction:

> The Rigveda is ancient because of its **language and textual tradition**, not because the particular script seen in a modern manuscript is equally ancient.

The Devanagari manuscript shown in the source is an **early-19th-century manuscript**, not a manuscript from the Vedic period.

---

# 36. The Rigveda in modern Hinduism

The Rigveda continues to have a living presence.

Some of its hymns are still recited during:

* weddings,
* funerals,
* initiations,
* and other rites of passage.

The wedding hymn **10.85**, for example, remains part of Hindu wedding rituals.

The **Devī Sūkta**, hymn 10.125, is connected with the goddess tradition and is recited during **Durga Puja**.

The Rigveda is therefore not merely an archaeological text.

Some of its verses continue to be used in religious ceremonies thousands of years after their composition.

At the same time, modern scholars note that most contemporary Hindus do not engage with the literal content of the Vedic hymns in the same way ancient Vedic specialists did.

For many people, the Vedas function primarily as a symbol of ancient religious and cultural heritage.

Musicians and dancers have also incorporated Rigvedic material into later artistic traditions, including Carnatic compositions such as those associated with **Hamsadhvani** and **Śubhapaṇṭuvarāḷi**.

---

# 37. The Rigveda and modern debates about Indian history

The Rigveda has become central to modern debates about the origins of Indian civilization.

Some modern theories, including **Indigenous Aryan** and **Out-of-India** theories, attempt to date the Rigveda to the period of, or even before, the Indus Valley Civilization.

From that premise, some have argued that the Indus Valley Civilization itself was Vedic or Aryan.

These claims are controversial and are **not accepted by mainstream scholarship**.

For example, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**, in *Orion* (1893), used astronomical arguments concerning the position of the constellation Orion to propose a Rigvedic date as early as **6000–4000 BCE**.

These very early dates are not accepted in mainstream scholarship.

---

# 38. The central historical picture

The safest way to understand the Rigveda is therefore to imagine a long process rather than a single moment.

### Stage 1 — Composition

Poets composed hymns in an ancient form of **Indo-Aryan/Vedic Sanskrit**, probably mainly between about **1500 and 1000 BCE**, although earlier and wider dating proposals exist.

### Stage 2 — Oral preservation

The hymns were memorized and transmitted through extremely precise oral techniques.

The sound of the text was itself sacred and important.

### Stage 3 — Organization

Different clans and priestly schools preserved their own collections.

During the Kuru period, these traditions were brought together and organized into the developing Rigvedic canon.

### Stage 4 — Standardization

The pronunciation, *sandhi*, Padapāṭha and other aspects of the text were increasingly fixed.

### Stage 5 — Manuscripts

Much later, the text began to appear in written manuscripts.

These manuscripts used different regional scripts.

### Stage 6 — Devanagari editions

Modern editions frequently use **Devanagari**, which is why an ancient Vedic hymn today may look like:

**अग्निमीळे पुरोहितं...**

But those letters are **not the letters used when the hymn was composed**.

They are a much later visual representation of an ancient text.

---

# 39. The most important distinction

The Rigveda contains three things that should not be confused:

**1. The language**

> Vedic Sanskrit

**2. The text**

> Ancient hymns transmitted through an extraordinarily precise oral tradition

**3. The script**

> A much later method of representing that ancient language in writing

So when you see the Rigveda printed in Devanagari, you are effectively looking at:

> **an ancient oral Sanskrit composition represented through a much later writing system.**

That is why there is no contradiction between the Rigveda being more than 3,000 years old and the manuscript in front of you being written in Devanagari.

The **text is ancient**.

The **script is comparatively recent**.

And the fact that the text survived at all is largely due not to manuscripts, but to the extraordinary oral tradition that preserved it for centuries before the surviving manuscripts were produced.
